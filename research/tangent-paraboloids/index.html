Research explained · Geometric analysis

Beyond
tangent planes.

A tangent describes the shape we see when we zoom in. Can the speed of that approximation tell us how smoothly a set is built?

Francesco Paolo Maiale · Based on joint work with Kennedy Obinna Idu

Read the paperExplore the idea ↓The result ↓

01 · A different geometry

Zooming in
changes the ruler.

Think of a curve drawn on paper. Near an ordinary smooth point, a short section looks almost straight. The tangent line captures its first-order shape; the remaining deviation carries more information.

Our setting is a Heisenberg group. Its natural magnification scales horizontal coordinates by r and the central coordinate by r2. Distances and tangent models must respect this geometry. For the surfaces in our paper, the tangent model is a vertical homogeneous subgroup: a subgroup preserved by these dilations and containing the central direction. See §2 of the paper.

02 · The idea

Measure the error.
Then its rate.

Suppose a nearby point is at distance r from our base point. Compare its distance from the tangent model with r itself. A linear allowance, proportional to r, keeps the same relative thickness at every scale.

An allowance proportional to r1+α, with 0 < α ≤ 1, shrinks faster. After dividing by r, its relative thickness is proportional to rα, which tends to zero. This is the narrowing envelope in the figure.

Try the scaling

Take α = ½ and constant one. At scale r = ¼, the error allowance is ⅛: half the observation scale. At r = 1/16, it is 1/64: just a quarter of the scale. Each fourfold zoom halves the relative allowance.

What is the paraboloid?

For a base point p, tangent subgroup V and constant λ > 0, the envelope contains points q satisfying

d(p−1q,V)≤λd(p,q)1+α.

This is Qα(p, V, λ) in Definition 3.1. The distance is intrinsic. “Paraboloid” names this power-law envelope; the exponent is two only when α = 1.

03 · The result

Local information.
Smooth pieces.

Our main theorem gives a sufficient condition for covering a set, apart from a measure-zero remainder, by countably many intrinsically CH1,α regular surfaces. This property is called higher-order rectifiability.

The test combines concentration around a suitable envelope at almost every point with a positive lower density assumption and finite measure. Concentration is measured by Hausdorff measure, the notion of size associated with the intrinsic distance.

The precise scope and assumptions

Fix 0 < α ≤ 1 and n < k ≤ 2n. Let E ⊂ ℍn be measurable with finite ℋk+1 measure and positive lower (k+1)-density almost everywhere. At almost every p ∈ E, assume there are a vertical k-dimensional subgroup Vp and some λp > 0 such that

ℋk+1(E∩B(p,r)∖Qα(p,Vp,λp))rk+1→0as r↓0.

Then countably many intrinsic CH1,α regular k-surfaces cover E up to an ℋk+1-null set. The metric dimension is k+1: in ℍ1, this covers k = 2 with ℋ3. Positive lower density is a separate hypothesis in Theorem 1.1.

Here regularity concerns horizontal derivatives, with α controlling their Hölder continuity. The conclusion allows many pieces and an exceptional null set. It does not claim one globally smooth Euclidean surface; even α = 1 gives intrinsic CH1,1 regularity, rather than twice differentiability.
