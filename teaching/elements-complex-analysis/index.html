Before you begin

A little background.

Bring complex differentiation, power series, contour integration, and the Cauchy integral formula. Some point-set topology and metric-space compactness are useful for the convergence section. If these prerequisites are new, pause to work concrete contour and power-series examples before pursuing the functional-space language.

A first pass

Read the developed one-variable material.

These are partial notes from the opening lectures. The available pages develop one-variable complex analysis. A later part heading announces several complex variables, but no developed chapter follows it; the guide therefore stays within the material actually present.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Section 1.1 · pp. 5–6

    Recall what holomorphicity buys you

    Connect complex differentiability, local power series, and the Cauchy formula. Keep a simple polynomial and a rational function beside the definitions, marking where each is holomorphic and where the domain matters.

    Keep in mind The domain is part of every assertion about a holomorphic function.

  2. Section 1.2 · pp. 7–13

    Choose the right convergence

    Distinguish pointwise convergence from uniform convergence on compact subsets. Follow Weierstrass, Montel, and Vitali by recording their assumptions and their different conclusions about limits or subsequences.

    Keep in mind A convergence theorem needs the appropriate topology and bounds.

  3. Section 1.3 · pp. 14–18

    Classify singular behaviour

    Use Laurent expansions to distinguish removable singularities, poles, and essential singularities. Then interpret meromorphic functions as maps to the Riemann sphere, comparing what happens in a coordinate near infinity.

    Keep in mind An infinite value on the sphere and an essential singularity are different phenomena.

  4. Section 1.4 · pp. 18–20

    Turn local information into a count

    Read the residue theorem, argument principle, and Rouché’s theorem as a connected chain. For each contour, draw its orientation, mark singularities, and check the boundary assumptions before computing.

    Keep in mind Multiplicity and winding number carry information that a sketch alone can miss.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why is locally uniform convergence so useful?

Apply the Cauchy integral formula on a closed disk contained in the domain. Ask how uniform convergence on the integration contour controls the limit and its derivatives inside.

How do you recognise a pole from a Laurent series?

Inspect the negative powers and identify whether the principal part is finite. Compare this with a removable singularity and with infinitely many negative terms.

What must be checked before applying Rouché’s theorem?

Choose the comparison function and verify the strict inequality on the boundary. Then state precisely which zeros are being counted, including multiplicities.
