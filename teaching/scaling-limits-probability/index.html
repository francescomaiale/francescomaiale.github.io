Before you begin

A little background.

Bring measure-theoretic probability, weak convergence of measures, ordinary differential equations, and some familiarity with Brownian motion and Itô calculus. For the continuum arguments, basic PDEs and semigroup methods help. Chapter 1 recalls several stochastic ingredients, but it is easier to follow with those ideas already in view.

A first pass

Follow the empirical measure.

Read Chapters 1–2 with one question in mind: how can the positions of many particles become an equation for a density? Keep the normalisation, interaction strength, and noise scale visible in your own notation.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapter 1 · pp. 7–18

    Name the microscopic objects

    Distinguish an individual particle trajectory, the full interacting system, its empirical measure, and a smoothed empirical density. Rework the test-function interpretation before using any limit argument.

    Keep in mind A random empirical measure and a deterministic density solve different kinds of problems.

  2. Chapters 2–3 · pp. 19–58

    Derive a candidate continuum equation

    Trace the empirical-measure identity through Itô’s formula, then separate the formal limiting calculation from the estimates that justify it. Use the cloud and rain-droplet models to see how modelling choices alter the equation.

    Keep in mind A plausible PDE is the beginning of a convergence argument, rather than its conclusion.

  3. Chapter 4 · pp. 59–78

    Understand propagation of chaos

    Compare the interacting particles with the mean-field SDE. Track the coupling estimate and the role of Wasserstein distance, then ask what the result says about a fixed number of particles as the total number grows.

    Keep in mind Independence in the limit must be formulated precisely.

  4. Chapters 5–7 · pp. 79–128

    Compare other limits

    Read the later models selectively: singular and local interactions, lattice exclusion, then stochastic transport. Before each calculation, write down what is being rescaled and which quantity is expected to converge.

    Keep in mind Changing the interaction or noise scale can change the limiting equation.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why introduce an empirical measure?

Apply it to a test function and compare the result with an average over particle positions. This is the bridge from an N-particle system to a measure-valued description.

Where does a formal derivation become a proof?

Locate an estimate controlling the difference between the finite system and the candidate limit. Check the topology, the assumptions on the drift, and the sense of convergence.

Does propagation of chaos say that finite particles never interact?

Separate the interacting finite system from the limiting joint law of a fixed number of particles. The assertion concerns an asymptotic distribution, with hypotheses supporting it.
