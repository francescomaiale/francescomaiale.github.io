Before you begin

A little background.

Start Chapter 1 with multivariable calculus and integration by parts. For the existence theory, it helps to know measure theory, functional analysis, Sobolev spaces, and weak convergence.

A first pass

Begin with Chapters 1–2.

First learn how a functional produces an equation; then ask why a minimiser exists. Return to the later chapters as the need for weaker formulations and limiting energies becomes clearer.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapter 1 · pp. 5–30

    From an energy to an equation

    Follow the first-variation examples. Pay attention to admissible perturbations, integration by parts, and the difference between fixed boundary values and natural boundary conditions.

    Keep in mind A stationary point need not be a minimiser.

  2. Chapter 2 · pp. 31–60

    Why should a minimiser exist?

    Follow a minimising sequence through coercivity, compactness, and lower semicontinuity. Track the topology and check that the limit remains admissible.

    Keep in mind Obtaining a limit and passing the energy to that limit are separate steps.

  3. Chapters 3–4 · pp. 61–92

    When the original problem changes

    Compare the convexity conditions, then study relaxation. Ask what a sequence of increasingly fine competitors can achieve, and how a relaxed functional records that behaviour.

    Keep in mind The choice of topology affects which limits are visible.

  4. Chapters 5–6 · pp. 93–120

    Weak equations and limiting energies

    Revisit first variations in Sobolev spaces, then move to Γ-convergence. Use the phase-transition example to connect the abstract definition with a model.

    Keep in mind A weak Euler–Lagrange equation and a limit of energies answer different questions.

For a geometric continuation, Chapters 7–8 (pp. 121–146) discuss rearrangements and isoperimetric inequalities. Chapter 8 records lectures by Aldo Pratelli.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

What distinguishes a stationary point from a minimiser?

Start with a one-variable function. A vanishing derivative can occur at a maximum too. Then ask what convexity and the admissible class add to the variational argument.

Which perturbations are allowed at the boundary?

For fixed boundary values, the perturbation must preserve those values. With a free boundary trace, keep the boundary term after integration by parts and see which natural condition it gives.

Where could the direct method fail?

Separate the steps: a useful bound, a convergent subsequence, an admissible limit, and lower semicontinuity of the energy. Identify the assumption supporting each step.

A sign convention to check on p. 8

For the energy written there as the Dirichlet energy plus a potential, stationarity gives −Δu + fu = 0. The potential sign in the displayed equations (1.5)–(1.6) should agree with that derivation.
