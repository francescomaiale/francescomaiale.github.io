Before you begin

A little background.

Use linear algebra over the real and complex numbers, eigenvalues, matrix products, and elementary differential calculus. Basic groups and topology help with the opening chapters and covering-space discussion. The physics applications are easier with some quantum mechanics, but the mathematical route can be followed by concentrating on representations and examples.

A first pass

Use SU(2) as a running example.

Return to its matrices, Lie algebra, topology, and representations as each viewpoint appears. This gives a common reference point across a long set of notes. Choose later physics examples according to your interests rather than treating every application as a prerequisite.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–2 · pp. 8–35

    Connect groups with infinitesimal symmetries

    Start with concrete matrix groups. Compute a few commutators and distinguish a group element from an element of its Lie algebra. Read the adjoint representation as another way the symmetry acts.

    Keep in mind Local Lie-algebra information and global group topology answer different questions.

  2. Chapters 3–5 · pp. 36–60

    Build the representation toolkit

    Read the fundamental-group and covering-space examples, then the sections on Haar measure and representations. In Schur’s lemmas, identify irreducibility and the field before using a conclusion.

    Keep in mind A representation is an action by linear maps; choosing a basis changes its matrices.

  3. Chapters 6–11 · pp. 62–120

    Work one family before comparing many

    Begin with SU(2), then compare SU(3) or an orthogonal group. Return later to the Euclidean, Lorentz, and Poincaré examples when their physical interpretation is useful. Track the representation space and its dimension throughout.

    Keep in mind Equivalent representations can look different, while distinct groups can share local features.

  4. Chapter 12 · pp. 122–136

    Organise representations with roots and weights

    Read the Cartan-basis commutators alongside the weight diagrams. Reconstruct a small diagram before using the notation of simple roots, Dynkin diagrams, and Cartan matrices. Keep the rank distinct from the dimension of a representation.

    Keep in mind A diagram compresses algebraic data only after its conventions are understood.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

How do you check that a matrix-valued map is a representation?

Verify that multiplication is respected, then check the identity and inverse. Separately ask whether a nontrivial invariant subspace exists.

Why compare SU(2) with SO(3)?

Separate their Lie algebras from their global topology and covering relation. Ask which statement concerns local rotations and which concerns the group as a whole.

What does a weight diagram record?

Identify a commuting collection of generators and their simultaneous eigenvalues. Then connect the raising and lowering operations with movements between weights.
