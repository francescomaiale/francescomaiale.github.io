Teaching & Notes/Quantitative stochastic homogenization

Lecture-note reading guide

Stochastic
homogenization.

These handwritten notes introduce quantitative questions about elliptic equations in random media. The central reading thread is how local energy minimisation can lead to an effective description at a larger scale.

Before you begin

A little background.

Review weak elliptic equations, quadratic variational problems, probability, and expectation. Recognise stationary random coefficients and distinguish random quantities from deterministic effective coefficients.

A first pass

Keep the energy minimisation problem nearby.

Start with variational quantities on a bounded domain with affine boundary data. Track the domain, slope, and coefficient field. The linked course book helps when these compact notes move quickly.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Variational starting point

    Identify the microscopic problem

    Follow the definition of the energy with prescribed affine boundary values. Derive its weak Euler–Lagrange equation and compare the minimiser with the affine competitor. First try a constant coefficient field as a reference example.

    Keep in mind The minimum depends on both the domain and the imposed macroscopic slope.

  2. Subdividing domains

    See why scale matters

    In the arguments using partitions into smaller domains, track how local competitors produce an inequality for the larger domain. Distinguish the deterministic variational inequality from the use of stationarity after taking expectations.

    Keep in mind An energy comparison and a probabilistic averaging step have different justifications.

  3. Effective coefficients

    Read the quadratic forms geometrically

    Follow the coefficient matrices and their quadratic forms through the comparison estimates. Instead of treating every matrix identity as formal algebra, ask what it says about the energy required for a chosen slope.

    Keep in mind The effective object describes the large-scale response; it does not replace the coefficient field pointwise.

  4. Quantitative estimates

    Separate the rate from the random factor

    For the homogenization estimate, identify the small scale parameter, the deterministic exponent, and the random quantity controlling the error. Return to the energy estimates to understand what supports each term.

    Keep in mind Convergence alone and a bound on how fast convergence occurs are different conclusions.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why is the constant-coefficient case a useful test?

With affine boundary values, check whether the affine function solves the weak equation. Compute its energy and compare it with the variational definition.

Where is randomness used?

Mark the first step that takes an expectation or invokes stationarity. Compare it with the preceding energy inequalities, which can be read for a fixed coefficient field.

What makes an estimate quantitative?

Locate the scale parameter and a power that tends to zero. Then identify which constants or random factors are controlled, rather than simply asserting convergence.
