Before you begin

A little background.

You should be comfortable with topological spaces, continuous maps, quotient constructions, and elementary groups. Prior exposure to the fundamental group will help: the opening chapter recalls it while moving toward higher homotopy groups. Draw the domains and mark base points whenever a definition feels too compressed.

A first pass

Treat this as an opening fragment.

The library records only the first few lectures. Read the developed homotopy material first, then use the simplicial definitions as an introduction. The chapter title mentions homology, but the available text does not develop a full homology theory.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Sections 1.1–1.2 · pp. 4–7

    Identify maps and allowed deformations

    Compare homotopy, homotopy equivalence, retraction, and the elementary categorical language. For each construction, sketch the spaces and arrows, then state what must remain fixed throughout the deformation.

    Keep in mind Homotopy equivalence preserves a different amount of information from homeomorphism.

  2. Sections 1.3–1.4 · pp. 8–13

    Make the base point explicit

    Follow the definition using maps from a cube with its boundary collapsed to a chosen point. Compare the alternative descriptions and track how a continuous map between spaces induces a map between homotopy groups.

    Keep in mind The boundary condition is part of the object being defined.

  3. Section 1.5 · pp. 14–15

    Read a relative construction

    Write down the pair of spaces before following the relative homotopy groups and long exact sequence. Concentrate first on the source and target of each arrow, then on what exactness means at one term.

    Keep in mind A useful diagram tells you which data are retained and which are forgotten.

  4. Chapter 2 · pp. 16–22

    Move between combinatorics and geometry

    Compare an abstract simplex with its geometric realisation. Work through faces and orientation for an interval and a triangle before reading the definition of a simplicial complex. Stop at the scope of the available text.

    Keep in mind These are foundations for later homology, rather than a substitute for its missing development.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Can spaces be homotopy equivalent without being homeomorphic?

Compare a point with a contractible space such as the real line. Identify a contraction and then ask whether a bijective homeomorphism could exist.

What does a based homotopy preserve?

Draw the boundary of the cube and follow its image throughout the deformation. Check which conditions would disappear if the base-point requirement were dropped.

What makes a family of simplices a complex?

Begin with a filled triangle and list its faces. Then check both the closure under faces and the intersection condition in a geometric realisation.
