Before you begin

A little background.

Bring one-variable complex analysis, topology of surfaces, linear algebra, and elementary algebra. Familiarity with differential forms will help in the middle chapters. The title is broad, but the central objects here are Riemann surfaces and algebraic curves: keep a sphere and a complex torus as reference examples.

A first pass

Follow meromorphic functions across viewpoints.

Ask how local zeros and poles become a divisor, how that divisor defines a space of sections, and how Riemann–Roch controls its dimension. This question gives the later sheaf language a concrete purpose before the formal constructions become demanding.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–4 · pp. 7–42

    Build geometric examples

    Follow the definitions of Riemann surfaces and holomorphic maps, then work with the sphere and torus. Track the degree of a map and the branch behaviour in the Hurwitz formula. Keep local coordinates distinct from the surface itself.

    Keep in mind Local holomorphic descriptions must agree on overlaps.

  2. Chapters 5–6 · pp. 44–62

    Learn why local data need bookkeeping

    Read differential forms and integration before approaching sheaves and exact sequences. Give each abstract definition an example: holomorphic functions, meromorphic functions, or local forms. Identify the restriction maps and the gluing condition.

    Keep in mind A sheaf records compatible local information; cohomology measures more than a collection of formulas.

  3. Chapters 7–8 · pp. 64–93

    Translate divisors into dimensions

    Compute simple divisors of meromorphic functions and compare principal divisors with arbitrary divisors. Follow the associated sheaves into Riemann–Roch and Serre duality, writing down the terms in the dimension formula before the proof.

    Keep in mind The degree of a divisor is a useful invariant, but does not determine every space of sections by itself.

  4. Chapters 9–10 · pp. 94–133

    Return to global geometry

    Use the applications of Riemann–Roch to understand maps into projective space. Then read the Picard group, Jacobian, and Abel–Jacobi construction as successive ways of organising divisor information. Revisit a low-genus example whenever possible.

    Keep in mind A global equivalence relation among divisors carries information that local multiplicities alone cannot show.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

What does the divisor of a meromorphic function record?

List its zeros and poles with signed multiplicities. On a compact surface, compare the sum of those orders with the degree of the resulting principal divisor.

Why introduce sheaves instead of one global formula?

Choose local coordinate charts and ask which functions or forms are defined on each. Check how restriction and gluing express compatibility without requiring a global coordinate.

What does Riemann–Roch let you count?

Identify the divisor, its degree, the genus, and the spaces appearing in the formula. Work a simple example before concluding which meromorphic functions can exist.
