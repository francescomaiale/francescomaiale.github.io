Before you begin

A little background.

You will get most from these notes with linear algebra, multivariable calculus, and some experience solving small problems on a computer. Review eigenvalues, orthogonal projections, gradients, and Hessians before the optimization part.

A first pass

Follow the error, not just the formula.

Begin with Chapters 1–3, then choose a linear-algebra or optimization route. Keep a small, nearly singular matrix nearby: it makes the difference between an exact identity and a reliable computation much easier to see.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–3 · pp. 9–46

    Build an error vocabulary

    Connect vector and matrix norms with the singular value decomposition, least squares, and conditioning. In Chapter 3, separate rounding errors introduced by an algorithm from sensitivity already present in the problem.

    Keep in mind A small residual and a small solution error are different statements.

  2. Chapters 4–5 · pp. 47–80

    Choose a factorization

    Compare LU with pivoting, Cholesky, and QR by checking their assumptions and the problem they solve. For least squares, follow the geometry of the orthogonal projection before comparing the formulas.

    Keep in mind A method suited to a square system may be a poor choice for least squares.

  3. Chapter 6 · pp. 81–110

    Compute spectral information

    Read eigenvalue conditioning before QR iteration and the power methods. Draw what an iteration does to components along different eigendirections, then inspect how a shift changes the calculation.

    Keep in mind Convergence depends on spectral structure as well as the starting vector.

  4. Chapters 8–10 · pp. 119–173

    Move from solving to optimizing

    Follow a quadratic objective through descent, Newton methods, line search, and constraints. When a method is introduced, identify its direction, step rule, and stopping test separately.

    Keep in mind A locally useful search direction still needs a sensible step length.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

How could a tiny residual be misleading?

Use a matrix with a very small singular value. An error in the corresponding direction can be large even when multiplying it by the matrix gives something small. Relate the example to conditioning.

Why compare QR and normal equations for least squares?

Start from their shared minimization problem, then compare the matrices that must be handled numerically. Think about how singular values change when the matrix is multiplied by its transpose.

What should you record in a numerical experiment?

Keep the problem, initialization, tolerance, residual, and iteration count. Compare the observed error with the theoretical assumptions instead of treating an attractive convergence plot as a complete explanation.

From these notes to my research

Finding the slowest matrix growth

Matrix norms, spectral information, and reliable numerical estimates form a useful starting point for my work with Nicola Guglielmi on the lower spectral radius. The problem moves from a single matrix to products chosen from a nonnegative matrix family. The algorithm searches for slowly growing products while refining lower and upper bounds.

As you exploreTrack which calculation supplies an upper bound and which supplies a lower bound. Try the corrected MATLAB example, then compare the remaining gap with the stopping tolerance and search budget.
