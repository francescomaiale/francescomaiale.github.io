Dispersive estimates
& applications.

These handwritten summer-school notes study how solutions of evolution equations spread and how that spreading leads to useful estimates. Begin with the pictures and model equations, then connect them to the norms appearing in the proofs.

Before you begin

A little background.

Bring Fourier analysis, Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, and basic linear wave and Schrödinger equations. Interpolation and duality become important when moving from individual estimates to space-time bounds.

A first pass

Start with a moving wave packet.

Read the opening wave and Schrödinger comparison before the abstract operator estimates. Sketch where a packet is concentrated and how it evolves. Distinguish spatial spreading from loss of a conserved norm.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Model equations and dispersion

    Give the estimates a picture

    Follow the opening examples, the Gaussian packet, and the plane-wave calculation. Compare the dispersion relations for the wave and Schrödinger equations, and ask what they suggest about the motion of different frequencies.

    Keep in mind A packet spreading in space need not lose its conserved L² norm.

  2. Pointwise and space-time information

    Read the norms before the proof

    Compare pointwise decay with mixed time-space estimates. Write out which variable each exponent belongs to, and keep the required regularity of the initial data beside the conclusion.

    Keep in mind A pointwise bound and an integrated space-time bound answer different questions.

  3. Strichartz estimates

    Keep track of admissible exponents

    Use the exponent diagrams and the statements of the estimates as a map through the interpolation and duality arguments. Note the dimension and any endpoint restrictions before trying to apply a formula to a new pair of exponents.

    Keep in mind An endpoint is a separate issue; it is not automatically included by approaching it with other exponents.

  4. Forcing terms and further estimates

    Distinguish free and forced evolution

    When the time integral of a propagator appears, identify the forcing term and compare the estimate with the homogeneous evolution. Read the later weighted estimates by recording the weight and the assumptions under which it is useful.

    Keep in mind The source term introduces its own norm and time integration, not just another initial datum.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Can an amplitude decay while an L² norm stays constant?

Imagine a packet becoming wider and lower. Compare its maximum height with the integral of its squared magnitude; this separates dispersion from dissipation.

What does a mixed time-space norm measure?

First take the spatial norm at a fixed time, then take a time norm of the resulting function. Check the order before comparing two estimates.

What changes when a forcing term is added?

Separate the freely propagated initial state from the integral of propagated sources. Identify which estimate controls each contribution and which exponents are required.
