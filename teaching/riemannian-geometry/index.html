Before you begin

A little background.

Start with multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and basic ordinary differential equations. It helps to have met smooth manifolds, but the opening pages recall charts, tangent spaces, vector fields, and differential forms.

A first pass

Read this as an opening chapter.

The PDF covers only the first third of the course. My suggested route stays within that excerpt: learn to distinguish an intrinsic object from its coordinate expression, then follow this distinction into geodesic calculations.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Opening discussion · PDF p. 2

    Make tangent vectors concrete

    Compare tangent vectors described by curve velocities with tangent vectors described as derivations. Work first on a plane or sphere, and ask what information depends on the chosen coordinates.

    Keep in mind Coordinates describe a vector; they are not the vector itself.

  2. Metric examples · PDF p. 8

    Measure on a surface

    Use the surface-of-revolution calculation to see how a parametrization pulls the ambient metric back to coordinates. Sketch the coordinate directions and identify the lengths and angles the coefficients encode.

    Keep in mind A metric allows measurements without requiring a preferred parametrization.

  3. Parallel transport · PDF pp. 14 and 20

    Differentiate along a curve

    Follow the covariant derivative and the condition defining a parallel vector field. Then compare the Euclidean example with the exercise on the sphere, where the chosen path becomes significant.

    Keep in mind A vector field may change its coordinate components while remaining parallel.

  4. Length and local minimality · PDF pp. 26 and 32

    Relate geodesics to distance

    Read the length discussion alongside the exponential-map argument. Mark where the reasoning is local, and keep the hypotheses of the minimizing statement visible when you draw an example.

    Keep in mind Being a geodesic does not automatically imply global minimality between distant endpoints.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

What survives a change of coordinates?

Choose a tangent vector and express it in two charts. Its components change, but its action on a smooth function describes the same geometric derivative. Use the chain rule to connect the two descriptions.

How would you read a metric matrix geometrically?

Apply the matrix to two coordinate vectors. The diagonal terms determine their squared lengths, while the off-diagonal term records their inner product. Try the surface-of-revolution example before a more general surface.

Why distinguish local and global shortest paths?

Think of geodesic arcs on a sphere. Compare a short arc with a longer continuation along the same great circle. The differential equation is local, while a global comparison involves all competing curves.
