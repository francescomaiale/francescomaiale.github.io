Before you begin

A little background.

Review probability measures, push-forwards, weak convergence, and convex functions. Begin with Optimal Transport I; manifolds and geodesics help with the geometric material.

A first pass

Keep one transport problem in view.

Choose two probability measures and ask how a map or plan moves one into the other. These course notes are partial: gaps between lectures may require additional reading.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Measures and admissibility

    Write down the objects first

    Begin with the measure preliminaries and push-forward formula. Distinguish a transport map from a transport plan, and check which marginal conditions express conservation of mass. Try the definitions on a measure supported at two points.

    Keep in mind A coupling is a measure on a product space, rather than necessarily the graph of a map.

  2. Convex analysis and duality

    Read the two problems together

    Read convex conjugates and duality alongside the transport problem. List the spaces in which the functions and measures live, and separate a dual bound from the conditions for equality.

    Keep in mind Duality provides information about the original minimisation problem through a different class of competitors.

  3. Geometric transport

    Compare Euclidean and manifold settings

    When transport on manifolds appears, pause to recall distance, geodesics, and the exponential map. Compare the geometric form of the optimal map with the Euclidean picture before working through the proof details.

    Keep in mind The cost and the geometry of the underlying space influence the form of a transport map.

  4. Curves and action

    Track what changes with time

    For curves in metric spaces, distinguish the curve itself, its speed, and the action built from that speed. Sketch a simple path with constant speed, then ask how a different parametrisation affects the quantities being estimated.

    Keep in mind An estimate between two times and a statement about the whole curve carry different information.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

How can a plan split mass when a map cannot?

Draw one source point and two target points with prescribed masses. Describe an admissible measure on pairs, then ask whether a single-valued map from that source can produce the same target.

What makes a dual function admissible?

Write the inequality involving the cost before discussing an optimiser. Integrate it against an admissible plan to see why it gives a bound on the transport cost.

What should remain visible in a geometric example?

Identify the source and target measures, the cost, and the underlying distance. Only then introduce the potential and its associated map.
