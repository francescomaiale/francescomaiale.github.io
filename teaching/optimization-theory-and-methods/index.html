Before you begin

A little background.

A first course in linear algebra and multivariable calculus is enough to begin. Review gradients, Hessians, directional derivatives, and basic convergence of sequences; keep simple one- and two-dimensional examples available throughout.

A first pass

Start with a picture of the feasible set.

These notes identify themselves as work in progress. For a first reading, follow the route from a convex objective and a feasible region to an optimality condition, then ask how an algorithm can approach a solution.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–2 · pp. 5–27

    Recognize useful convexity

    Distinguish the optimization variable, objective value, and feasible region. Draw convex sets and compare convex, strictly convex, and strongly convex functions before using their differentiable characterizations.

    Keep in mind Existence, uniqueness, and a way to compute a solution are separate questions.

  2. Sections 2.5–2.8 · pp. 28–45

    Work without a smooth gradient

    Use the absolute-value function to interpret a subgradient as a supporting slope. Then connect subdifferentials, tangent cones, and convex conjugates by asking what each object says about the same graph or feasible set.

    Keep in mind A subdifferential can contain several vectors, even at a single point.

  3. Chapter 3 · pp. 46–54

    Read constraints geometrically

    Follow admissible directions into first-order optimality conditions. Compare unconstrained stationarity with the constrained case, then use the projection problem to make the role of the feasible set explicit.

    Keep in mind A direction that decreases the objective is useful only if the constraint permits it.

  4. Chapter 4 · pp. 55–73

    Turn a condition into an iteration

    Compare gradient steps, Armijo search, conjugate gradients, and Newton’s method on a quadratic. Keep the search direction, step length, and convergence assumptions in separate columns of your notes.

    Keep in mind A necessary optimality condition does not by itself establish convergence of an algorithm.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Can a convex problem fail to have a minimizer?

Try an open feasible interval or a function whose infimum is approached only at infinity. Convexity controls the shape of the problem; attainment still requires appropriate hypotheses.

What is the subdifferential of the absolute value at zero?

Draw every line through the origin that stays below the graph. The admissible slopes form an interval. Compare this picture with the single slope available away from the origin.

Why introduce a line search?

A descent direction describes the initial tendency of the objective. A step that is too large may overshoot that local decrease. Use a one-dimensional quadratic to distinguish these two decisions.
