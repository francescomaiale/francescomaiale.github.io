Before you begin

A little background.

Begin with convex sets and functions, supporting hyperplanes, subgradients, and elementary optimisation. Normed spaces, lower bounds, and basic convergence arguments will help in the central results. The seminar is written in Italian; keep a short glossary for the terms used in its definitions.

A first pass

Compare every extension with convexity.

This is my final-exam seminar associated with Giancarlo Bigi’s course, rather than a second set of course lectures. First understand which elementary supporting functions replace affine ones, then ask what separation and algorithmic arguments remain available.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapter 1 · pp. 5–14

    Change the family of supporting functions

    Read H-convexity through the familiar representation of a convex function as a supremum of affine minorants. Identify the chosen family H and compare the abstract subdifferential with the classical one.

    Keep in mind The supporting family is part of the definition, so changing it changes the theory.

  2. Chapter 2 · pp. 15–43

    Separate radiant geometry from convex geometry

    Draw examples of star-shaped and radiant sets before following the separation statements. Track the extra assumptions in the supporting-cone and positively homogeneous constructions, then connect them to the existence of abstract subgradients for calm functions.

    Keep in mind The seminar proves results under specific hypotheses; general nonconvex sets need not share them.

  3. Chapter 3, Section 3.1 · pp. 45–51

    Read a cutting method as a lower-bound argument

    Follow how supporting functions accumulate information about the objective. Identify the current approximation, the candidate point, and the assumptions used for convergence. Reproduce a small schematic iteration before reading the full proof.

    Keep in mind A support-based lower approximation and a feasible objective value have different roles.

  4. Chapter 3, Section 3.2 · pp. 51–54

    Understand why a region can be discarded

    Read the branch-and-bound construction with a partition of a simple interval or rectangle in mind. Track the lower bound on each region and the best available feasible value, then identify the comparison that justifies pruning.

    Keep in mind An attractive candidate alone is not a certificate that the remaining regions can be removed.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

What is generalised by H-convexity?

Start with the classical affine supporting functions and replace that family by H. Compare the resulting pointwise supremum and the meaning of touching from below.

Why distinguish radiant from convex sets?

Draw a set whose segments from the origin remain inside, while some segments joining other pairs of points do not. Check the definitions before applying a separation theorem.

What makes branch-and-bound reliable?

Separate a lower bound valid throughout a region from an upper bound obtained at one feasible point. Explain why their comparison can rule out an improvement.
