Before you begin

A little background.

Start with integration against measures, pushforwards, weak convergence, and basic convex analysis. Familiarity with compactness of probability measures is useful for existence. The preliminary section reviews notation; keep it beside you when a transport constraint or convergence argument becomes difficult.

A first pass

Begin with a mass-splitting example.

Compare transporting one concentrated source to two destinations with transporting a continuous density. That simple distinction explains why the notes move from Monge’s maps to Kantorovich’s plans before returning to conditions that produce an optimal map.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Sections 1.1–1.3 · pp. 5–18

    Separate maps from plans

    Check the pushforward constraint in Monge’s problem, then rewrite it as marginal constraints for a probability measure on a product space. Follow the existence proof while keeping admissibility, compactness, and the cost separate.

    Keep in mind Every transport map induces a plan; the reverse implication needs extra structure.

  2. Sections 1.4–1.6 · pp. 19–32

    Look for an optimality certificate

    Read duality alongside the definitions of c-transforms and c-cyclical monotonicity. Work through a one-dimensional example so that the inequalities describe actual pairings of source and destination points.

    Keep in mind Keep the cost and the sign conventions in the potential definitions visible.

  3. Section 1.7 · pp. 33–45

    Recover an optimal map

    Focus on the quadratic-cost theorem and list its hypotheses before following the proof. Return to the examples and applications to see how convex gradients, polar factorisation, and geometric inequalities fit together.

    Keep in mind Existence of an optimal plan and its concentration on a map are distinct conclusions.

  4. Section 1.8 · pp. 46–53

    Read the PDE through mass conservation

    Connect the Monge–Ampère equation with the transport identity and the change-of-variables formula. Treat the discussion of weaker regularity as a reason to revisit which derivatives and Jacobians are available.

    Keep in mind A formal density equation requires regularity assumptions before classical manipulations are justified.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

What additional freedom does a plan provide?

Try splitting the mass of a single atom between two target atoms. Compare a deterministic map with a joint measure having the prescribed marginals.

Why is cyclical monotonicity useful?

Imagine reassigning destinations around a finite cycle. The defining inequality says that this reassignment cannot lower the total cost on an optimal support.

Where is absolute continuity used?

Locate it in the quadratic-cost result and ask which conclusion could fail for an atomic source. Separate existence, uniqueness, and the representation by a convex gradient.
