Before you begin

A little background.

A working knowledge of Lebesgue integration, functional analysis, distributions, and Fourier transforms is helpful. For the second part, review smooth manifolds and tangent vectors; Appendix A provides a differential-geometric reference.

A first pass

Keep the Euclidean case beside you.

Use convolution and translation invariance as the connecting thread. First understand what is familiar on Euclidean space, then ask precisely which statements survive when the underlying group is noncommutative.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapter 1 · pp. 7–34

    Organize the Fourier toolkit

    Follow convolution through integrable functions, Schwartz functions, and tempered distributions. Keep a short table of the objects being transformed and the sense in which each identity is valid.

    Keep in mind A formal convolution needs hypotheses on integrability or supports.

  2. Chapters 2–3 · pp. 35–81

    From invariance to regularity

    Read translation-invariant operators and fundamental solutions before the discussion of hypoellipticity. In Hörmander’s theorem, identify the vector fields and their commutators in a concrete example.

    Keep in mind Missing directions can be recovered through commutators, rather than through ordinary ellipticity.

  3. Chapter 4 · pp. 83–99

    Learn the group geometry

    Translate familiar derivatives into the language of Lie algebras, the exponential map, and stratifications. Compare ordinary Euclidean dilations with the weighted dilations of a stratified algebra.

    Keep in mind The weights attached to different directions determine the relevant scaling.

  4. Chapter 5 · pp. 100–115

    Rebuild analysis on the group

    Track Haar measure, homogeneous dimension, convolution, and left-invariant differential operators. Keep the order of multiplication visible in every formula and compare it with the commutative case.

    Keep in mind Homogeneous dimension measures scaling and need not equal the number of coordinates.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why does the order in a convolution formula matter?

Write the group product and inverse explicitly before changing variables. In a noncommutative group, changing the order of two factors is an additional operation, not a harmless rearrangement.

How can a commutator reveal a new direction?

Choose two simple vector fields and compute their bracket on a test function. Compare the resulting direction with the pointwise span of the original fields.

Which dimension belongs in a scaling estimate?

Write how each coordinate changes under the group dilation, then compute how volume transforms. Compare that exponent with the ordinary dimension of the manifold.
