Before you begin

A little background.

Bring measure theory, pushforwards, convex functions, and basic multivariable calculus. Riemannian geometry helps in the second chapter, while the Heisenberg section introduces a different geometric setting. The opening transport examples are a useful entry point even if those later backgrounds need a separate review.

A first pass

Use the exercises as a reading rhythm.

Read one definition or theorem, attempt a nearby exercise, and only then consult the selected solutions in Chapter 3. This shorter set complements the longer transport course; it gives particular emphasis to the passage from transport to geometric inequalities.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Sections 1.1–1.2 · pp. 4–8

    Understand the relaxation

    Compare the mass-preserving map with a transport plan having prescribed marginals. Work through the examples where maps fail to exist or uniqueness fails, then use the exercises to test cyclical monotonicity.

    Keep in mind The choice of cost changes both the optimal arrangement and its possible uniqueness.

  2. Sections 1.3–1.4 · pp. 9–16

    Read potentials geometrically

    Connect the c-transform, contact set, and subdifferential before following the quadratic-cost and manifold transport results. State the assumptions before using a map or a Jacobian identity. Compare the Euclidean gradient picture with the Riemannian setting.

    Keep in mind A change-of-variables argument needs more than the existence of a measurable transport map.

  3. Chapter 2 · pp. 17–26

    See how transport proves inequalities

    Start with the Euclidean Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality and its interpolation of densities. Then follow distortion coefficients on manifolds before entering the Heisenberg example. At each transition, record which geometric ingredient changes.

    Keep in mind Curvature and the ambient geometry influence interpolation and volume distortion.

  4. Chapter 3 · pp. 27–34

    Use solutions to diagnose a gap

    Compare your argument with the selected exercise solutions. If an algebraic rearrangement is surprising, reduce it to two points or a one-dimensional example. Return to the original question and explain why the calculation addresses it.

    Keep in mind Reading a solution is most useful after identifying the step you could not justify.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why can an atom obstruct a transport map?

Try sending a single source atom to two different target atoms. A map assigns one image to the source point; a plan can distribute its mass.

What does cyclical monotonicity test?

Choose finitely many source–destination pairs and compare their total cost before and after permuting destinations. Work a two-point example with linear and quadratic cost.

What should remain visible in a geometric transport proof?

Identify the interpolation map, the transported densities, and the volume change. Then ask which estimate comes from the cost and which comes from the geometry of the space.
