Before you begin

A little background.

Bring measure theory, Lebesgue integration, basic functional analysis, and some familiarity with weak convergence. Smooth curves and surfaces are useful reference examples, but the purpose here is to go beyond smooth geometric objects.

A first pass

Keep a curve, a fractal, and a region in mind.

Compare what the definitions say about these three examples. Begin with Hausdorff measure and density, then ask when a rough set has an approximate tangent structure and when a region has a meaningful weak boundary.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–2 · pp. 6–33

    Choose a measure for the object

    Use Plateau’s problem as motivation, then follow measures, weak-star convergence, Hausdorff measure, and Cantor-type examples. Separate the dimension of a set from the value of its measure at that dimension.

    Keep in mind A notion of size should be matched to the geometric dimension being studied.

  2. Chapters 3–5 · pp. 35–70

    Look closely at small scales

    Read the covering theorems before the density results and self-similar sets. Draw the family of balls in a covering argument, and identify which disjointness or overlap control is actually used.

    Keep in mind Covering arguments turn local information into statements valid for almost every point.

  3. Chapters 7–8 · pp. 78–107

    Recover a tangent picture

    Follow Lipschitz differentiability and the area and coarea formulas into rectifiable sets and tangent bundles. Compare a smooth surface with an object described by countably many Lipschitz parametrizations.

    Keep in mind Rectifiability concerns structure up to a negligible set; it does not require a globally smooth surface.

  4. Chapters 9–10 · pp. 109–148

    Return to geometric minimization

    Move from functions of bounded variation to sets of finite perimeter. Track how compactness, lower semicontinuity, and boundary structure enter Plateau and capillarity problems rather than treating them as unrelated technical results.

    Keep in mind The distributional derivative of a characteristic function provides a weak notion of perimeter.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Why is Hausdorff dimension not the whole story?

Compare a line segment with a more complicated set having the same dimension. Ask which additional information is captured by measure, density, or rectifiability instead of dimension alone.

What is being counted in an area formula?

Begin with a parametrized curve that traces part of its image more than once. Distinguish measuring the image from integrating over the parameter domain, and look for the multiplicity term.

How does a set become a BV function?

Replace the set by its characteristic function. The distributional derivative records boundary behavior through a measure, allowing a perimeter to be defined even without a classical smooth boundary.

From these notes to my research

Beyond tangent planes

The notes connect Hausdorff measure, density, approximate tangents, and rectifiability in Euclidean space. My work with Kennedy Obinna Idu asks related questions in Heisenberg groups, where distances and dilations change the tangent models. Concentration around a narrowing power-law envelope helps reveal pieces with higher intrinsic regularity.

As you exploreCompare the tangent models and the measure used in each setting. Keep the density assumption separate from the rate of concentration around the tangent model.
