Before you begin

A little background.

You will need functional analysis, Sobolev spaces, and the basic variational formulation of a PDE. Review the distinction between a derivative, a gradient, and a weak equation before moving to infinite-dimensional critical-point arguments.

A first pass

Follow one energy throughout.

Choose a Dirichlet energy with a nonlinear potential and keep it beside you. Ask how its derivative is defined, how compactness is obtained, and which geometry might produce a critical point that direct minimization misses.

This is my suggested reading route, rather than an official course syllabus.

Where to start

A route through the notes.

  1. Chapters 1–3 · pp. 7–46

    Set up nonlinear analysis

    Compare Fréchet and Gâteaux derivatives, then use the Nemytskii-operator examples to check whether a pointwise formula defines a differentiable map on the chosen function space. Read inversion theorems with their hypotheses in view.

    Keep in mind A formal derivative still needs an appropriate domain and a continuity argument.

  2. Chapters 4–5 · pp. 48–71

    Identify the compactness mechanism

    Move from constrained critical points and Nehari manifolds to deformation arguments and Palais–Smale sequences. Separate the construction of a useful sequence from the step that makes it converge.

    Keep in mind An energy bound and a small derivative alone do not provide compactness without additional assumptions.

  3. Chapters 6–8 · pp. 72–111

    Find critical points through geometry

    Draw the mountain-pass picture before reading its proof. Then compare linking, category, and genus: identify the admissible families and the topological obstruction that prevents them from collapsing.

    Keep in mind The geometric picture suggests a critical value; compactness is what helps realize it.

  4. Chapters 9–10 · pp. 113–127

    Return to geometric models

    Read geodesics and Allen–Cahn energy as applications of the earlier machinery. For each example, identify the function space, energy, critical-point equation, and role of any limiting parameter.

    Keep in mind An application is a chance to reconstruct the abstract argument in a concrete setting.

Read actively

Three questions to carry with you.

Try answering these in your own words. Open a prompt when you want a starting point.

Where does a mountain-pass construction differ from minimization?

Compare the endpoint energy levels with the values attained along every joining path. The quantity being optimized is now the largest energy on a path, rather than simply the energy at an individual point.

What should you check for a Palais–Smale sequence?

Record the boundedness of the energy and the convergence of the derivative to zero. Then locate the theorem or estimate that yields a convergent subsequence in the required topology.

Why might symmetry give more critical points?

Look at the even-functional examples and the families allowed by the genus construction. Ask which symmetry is preserved and how it affects the admissible deformations, rather than counting solutions from a picture alone.
